# Editing English

Use these checks alongside the shared workflow. They address English phrasing,
not a separate editing mode or a list of forbidden words.

## Verbs and clause structure

Prefer a direct verb when an elaborate construction adds no meaning. "Is" and
"has" are often right; "serves as" is useful when an actual function matters.
Keep meaningful distinctions rather than mechanically replacing phrases.

Before:
> The archive serves as a repository for 800 letters. Additionally, it has the
> ability to provide visitors with access to scans of 200 of them.

After:
> The archive holds 800 letters. Visitors can view scans of 200 of them.

Check trailing participial clauses such as "highlighting," "underscoring," and
"ensuring." They often repeat an implication, obscure who acts, or introduce an
unsupported causal claim. Keep participles that express a real action or
relationship clearly.

Before:
> The team published the test results, making them available to all staff and
> highlighting its commitment to transparency. All staff can read the results.

After:
> The team published the test results for all staff to read.

Keep active voice when it clarifies responsibility. Use passive voice when the
actor is unknown or the result is the point. "The samples were frozen at −20°C"
does not need an invented researcher.

## Diction and continuity

Replace corporate abstractions with the action already described. Words such as
"leverage," "landscape," or "pivotal" may be imprecise in context, but they also
have legitimate uses. Do not replace them merely because they appear on a list.

Before:
> We leverage weekly calls to facilitate alignment across teams. During these
> calls, the teams agree on release dates.

After:
> Teams agree on release dates during weekly calls.

Let transitions explain a relationship. "However" can mark a real objection;
"additionally" is unnecessary when the next sentence simply continues the point.
Preserve standard technical terms instead of cycling through synonyms for variety.
Keep contractions, fragments, and informal diction when the voice supports them;
do not insert them into formal prose just to sound conversational.

## Punctuation and spelling

Preserve the source's consistent regional spelling, such as British or American
English. Use sentence case for generic headings unless the document has an
established convention or the heading contains proper names.

Apply compound hyphenation according to grammar and established spelling. "A
high-quality report" and "the report is high quality" differ, but some compounds
remain hyphenated after a noun: "the tool is self-contained." Do not strip every
predicative hyphen or invent hyphenated jargon.

Rhetorical dashes must be rewritten under the shared style constraints. Range
notation such as "2019–2024," the minus sign in "−20°C," and dashes inside exact
quotations remain intact. Straight and typographic quotation marks are both
valid; preserve the document's convention.

## Evidence and uncertainty

Simplifying a sentence must not improve its evidence by implication.

Before:
> According to the team's unpublished pilot, the tool could potentially reduce
> processing time by up to 12%, although the sample included only eight cases.

After:
> The team's unpublished pilot suggests the tool could cut processing time by
> up to 12%, but the sample included only eight cases.

The attribution, ceiling, uncertainty, and sample limitation all matter. If the
source says only "experts agree," do not invent a named expert, study, or result.
